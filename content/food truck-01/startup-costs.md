Opening a food truck costs less than opening most restaurants, but it is still a major investment. Costs vary widely depending on whether the truck is new or used, the local permit fees, and the menu.

## Major Expense Categories

| Category | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Vehicle** | Truck or trailer purchase, inspection | Usually the single largest cost |
| **Kitchen build-out** | Cooking equipment, refrigeration, sinks, hood | Must meet health and fire codes |
| **Permits and licenses** | Business license, health permit, fire inspection | See [[permits-and-licenses\|Permits and Licenses]] |
| **Insurance** | Commercial auto, general liability | Many events require proof of coverage |
| **Initial inventory** | Food, packaging, cleaning supplies | Enough for the first few weeks |
| **Branding** | Vehicle wrap, menu board, logo | Your truck is a moving billboard |
| **Technology** | Point-of-sale system, card reader | See [[point-of-sale-and-payments\|Point of Sale]] |
| **Working capital** | Cash reserve for slow weeks | Often overlooked by new owners |

## One-Time vs. Ongoing Costs

**One-time costs** are paid to open:
- Truck purchase and build-out
- Initial permits and inspections
- Branding and wrap

**Ongoing costs** come every week or month:
- Food and packaging
- Fuel and propane
- Commissary rent
- Insurance, loan payments, and permit renewals
- [[truck-maintenance|Maintenance and repairs]]

> [!warning] Don't skip the reserve
> Breakdowns and rainy weeks happen. A cash reserve keeps the business alive while the truck is in the shop or sales are slow.

## Ways to Reduce Startup Costs

1. Buy a **used truck** with a kitchen already installed
2. Start with a **smaller menu** that needs less equipment
3. **Lease** expensive equipment instead of buying it
4. Test the concept at **pop-ups** before committing to a truck
