A **business plan** turns a food truck idea into a clear roadmap. It forces you to answer hard questions early, and lenders or investors will usually ask to see one.

## Core Sections

The SBA describes a traditional business plan with these main parts:

1. **Executive summary:** What the business is and why it will succeed
2. **Company description:** Your concept, mission, and what makes you different
3. **Market analysis:** Customers, competitors, and local demand
4. **Organization and management:** Who runs the business and their roles
5. **Service or product line:** Your menu and how it's prepared
6. **Marketing and sales:** How customers will find you
7. **Funding request:** How much money you need and how it will be used
8. **Financial projections:** Expected sales, costs, and profit

## Food Truck Specifics to Include

Food trucks have details a standard plan might miss:

- **Route and location strategy:** Where and when you'll operate (see [[finding-locations|Finding Locations]])
- **Commissary arrangement:** Where food is prepped and stored (see [[prep-and-commissary|Prep and Commissary]])
- **Vehicle plan:** Buy new, buy used, or lease (see [[buying-a-truck|Buying a Truck]])
- **Seasonality:** How weather affects sales throughout the year

> [!example] One-sentence pitch
> "A breakfast taco truck serving commuters near three office parks on weekday mornings, and local breweries on weekends."

## Keep It Realistic

A plan is only useful if the numbers are honest. Build sales estimates from real observations, like how many customers a nearby truck serves per hour, rather than best-case guesses. Then compare them against your [[startup-costs|startup costs]].
