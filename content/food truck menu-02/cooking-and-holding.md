# Cooking and Holding on a Truck

A truck rarely cooks to order from raw. There is not enough equipment or time. Instead, most service runs on a par-cook and hold system: components are mostly cooked ahead, held at safe temperature, and finished when the ticket comes in. Getting that system right is the difference between a four-minute ticket and a fifteen-minute one.

## The Temperature Rules That Drive Everything

Food safety rules are not paperwork. They set the hard boundaries your whole service plan has to fit inside.

- Hot holding: **135°F or above**
- Cold holding: **41°F or below**
- The danger zone in between is where bacteria multiply fastest
- Cooling cooked food: down through the danger zone in stages, not by putting a full hot pan in the fridge

> Check your local health department's rules before you build your plan. Requirements and inspection standards vary by city and county, and the inspector's copy is the one that counts.

### Keep a Temp Log

Take temperatures on a schedule, not when you remember to. A simple sheet with the time, the item, and the reading does three jobs: it catches a failing hot well before you lose a whole pan, it shows an inspector you have a system, and it teaches you how your equipment actually behaves on a hot day versus a cold one.

## What Holds Well and What Does Not

Not every food survives holding. Learning this list from experience is expensive, so it is worth planning around.

### Holds Well

Braised and stewed proteins, chilis and sauces, beans, rice, cheese sauces, anything already cooked in liquid. These items often improve during a hold.

### Holds Badly

Anything fried, anything crisp, anything delicate. Fried food goes soft within minutes in a covered pan. Crisp vegetables wilt. If a crisp item is essential, it has to be cooked or finished to order, which means building the ticket time around it.

#### The Finish-to-Order Compromise

The usual solution is a split: hold the slow component, finish the fast one. The protein is held hot and portioned; the crisp element hits the fryer or the flat top when the ticket lands. The customer gets texture, and you do not lose the speed you built into [[building-a-core-menu]].

## Batch Cadence

Cook in batches sized to about thirty minutes of expected sales, not to the size of your biggest pan. Small batches mean the food spends less time holding, quality stays higher, and a slow day does not end with a full pan going in the trash. Overproduction is a direct hit to your numbers, which is worth understanding through [[costing-a-dish]].

Watch your recovery times too. A fryer dropped below temperature by a large batch needs minutes to come back, and during a rush those minutes stack up. That effect is covered in [[reading-the-line]].
