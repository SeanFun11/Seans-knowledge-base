# Specials and Rotation

A core menu should be stable. A special is how you stay interesting without touching it. Done well, specials bring regulars back, use up product you already have, and act as a low-risk testing ground for future menu items. Done badly, they quietly wreck your prep schedule and your ticket times.

## Why Run Specials at All

- **Regulars need a reason to return.** People who work near your regular stop may eat from you weekly. A rotating item gives them something new without a menu overhaul.
- **They move surplus.** Product that would otherwise be wasted becomes revenue.
- **They are auditions.** A special that sells consistently for a month is a proven candidate for the core menu.
- **They match the season.** Ingredients are cheaper and better at their peak, which helps the numbers in [[costing-a-dish]].

## The One-Pan Rule

The most useful constraint for a small kitchen is simple: a special may add one new prepped component, and no new equipment demand.

> If the special needs the fryer during the lunch rush, it is not a special, it is a second menu.

### Build on What Is Already Prepped

The best specials reuse core-menu components in a new arrangement. A held protein, a sauce you already make, and one new element becomes a new dish with almost no added prep. This is the cross-utilization idea from [[building-a-core-menu]] applied to a moving target.

## Cadence

Pick a schedule and hold it, because predictability is what makes people check.

1. **Weekly.** Strong for regular stops with repeat customers. Announce it the same day each week.
2. **Monthly.** Easier on prep and better for more involved items.
3. **Seasonal.** Three or four changes a year, tied to what is cheap and good.
4. **Event-only.** A special built for a specific festival or private booking.

### Track Every Special

Keep a running record with the date, the item, the number sold, the plate cost, and one line about how it ran during service. After six months that list is genuinely valuable: it tells you what sells, what your customers ignore, and which items slowed the window down, which connects back to [[reading-the-line]].

#### A Simple Retirement Rule

Decide in advance what failure looks like. For example: any special that sells fewer than a set number in two outings comes off, no discussion. Without a rule, weak items linger because someone on the truck likes them.

## Promote It the Day Before

A special nobody hears about is just extra prep. Post it the evening before with the item, the price, the stop, and the hours. Say plainly that it runs while supplies last, and then actually sell out rather than overproducing to avoid the word. Overproduction turns a profitable special into a loss, and the reasons why are covered in [[cooking-and-holding]].
