# Costing a Dish

Plenty of trucks are busy and still lose money. Usually the reason is not sales volume, it is that nobody ever costed the menu. Costing a dish means knowing exactly what one serving costs you before it reaches the window, so you can price it on purpose instead of by guessing what looks reasonable on a board.

![[assets/food-truck-service-window.jpg|500]]
*A food truck service window during lunch service. Every plate that goes through it should have a known cost behind it.*

## Start With Edible Portion Cost

The price on the invoice is not the cost of what you serve. Trim, peeling, bones, and cooking loss all shrink what you actually plate.

### An Example

If you buy ten pounds of a product for $40, that is $4.00 per pound as purchased. If trimming and cooking leave you with seven usable pounds, your real cost is about $5.71 per usable pound. Portion that at four ounces and the protein cost per serving is roughly $1.43.

Do this for every component: protein, starch, sauce, cheese, garnish. Add them up and you have the plate cost.

### Do Not Forget the Invisible Costs

These get left out constantly and they are not small:

- Containers, lids, cutlery, napkins, and bags
- Oil, seasoning, and cooking fats
- The portion of prep that ends up as waste

Packaging alone often runs fifty cents to over a dollar per order. On a $12 item, that is real money.

## Turning Cost Into Price

The standard tool is food cost percentage:

> **Menu price = plate cost ÷ target food cost percentage**

A common target range for food trucks is roughly 28 to 35 percent. If a plate costs $3.50 and you target 30 percent, the math gives $11.67, which you would round to $11.50 or $12.00.

#### Percentage Is Not the Whole Story

A cheap item with a great percentage can still be a poor performer. What pays your bills is contribution margin, which is price minus plate cost in actual dollars.

| Item | Price | Plate Cost | Food Cost % | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small side | $4.00 | $1.00 | 25% | $3.00 |
| Loaded entrée | $12.00 | $4.00 | 33% | $8.00 |

The side has the better percentage. The entrée puts nearly three times more money in the drawer per ticket. You want both on the menu, and you want to know which one you are pushing.

## Recost on a Schedule

Costs move. Re-run your numbers at least quarterly and any time a key ingredient jumps. Also remember that event fees, commissary rent, fuel, and propane come out of what is left, so a menu costed to the edge leaves nothing to absorb a slow day.

Costing also tells you which items deserve space on the board. An item that cannot be priced to work belongs in [[specials-and-rotation]] or nowhere. Portion control is what keeps these numbers honest during service, which ties directly to [[cooking-and-holding]] and to the discipline described in [[building-a-core-menu]].
