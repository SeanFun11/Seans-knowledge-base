# Building a Core Menu

A food truck menu is not a small restaurant menu. It is a menu shaped by three hard limits: the equipment bolted into the truck, the prep you can finish before the doors open, and the number of tickets you can push through the window in an hour. Most trucks that struggle are not bad at cooking. They are trying to run a twenty-item menu out of a kitchen the size of a hallway.

## Start With the Equipment, Not the Ideas

Before writing a single item, list what the truck actually has: burners, flat top, fryer, oven, hot wells, cold rail. Every menu item has to land on that list. If an item needs a piece of equipment you do not have, or needs the only burner during the lunch rush, it does not belong on the core menu.

> If two items fight over the same burner at noon, one of them is going to lose, and so is the customer waiting on it.

## Aim for Five to Eight Items

A tight menu is not a limitation, it is a strategy. Fewer items means:

- Less prep the night before
- Fewer ingredients to buy, store, and lose to spoilage
- Faster decisions at the window, which shortens the line
- Cooks who can run the whole menu from memory

### Use Cross-Utilization

Cross-utilization means one prepped ingredient shows up in several dishes. A braised protein can go in a sandwich, over a base, and into a loaded side. Three menu items, one prep task, one storage container. This is the single biggest lever a small kitchen has, and it also protects you: when one item sells out, the ingredients are already working somewhere else.

### Pick One Hero

Most successful trucks are known for one thing. The hero item is what people describe when they tell a friend about you. Build the rest of the menu to support it: an anchor item, two or three variations, one side, one thing for the person who does not eat what the hero is made of.

#### A Quick Test for Every Item

Ask four questions before an item earns a spot:

1. Can it be prepped ahead without falling apart?
2. Can it be finished and handed over in under four minutes?
3. Does it share at least one ingredient with another item?
4. Will it hold up in a container on the way home?

If an item fails two of these, it is a candidate for [[specials-and-rotation]] instead of the core menu.

## Write the Board Like a Sign, Not a Novel

The menu board is read from ten feet away by someone deciding whether to get in line. Item name, two to four words of description, price. Nothing else. Long descriptions slow the line down, and a slow line is the most expensive problem a truck has. For how that plays out during service, see [[reading-the-line]].

Once the item list is set, run every item through [[costing-a-dish]] before printing anything. It is common to discover that a favorite item cannot be priced to work, and it is much cheaper to learn that before the board is made.
