# Reading the Line

"Reading the line" means looking at the people waiting at your window and knowing, before the tickets reach you, what the next twenty minutes will demand. It is a skill built from paying attention, and it is what separates a rush that feels controlled from one that falls apart.

## Know Your Throughput Number

Every truck has a ceiling: the number of orders it can complete per hour at full effort. Find yours by counting tickets during a real rush and dividing by the time it took. Most single-window trucks land somewhere between 30 and 60 orders per hour depending on menu complexity.

That number is the most useful figure you own. With it, a line of forty people is not vague stress, it is roughly a forty-five minute wait, and you can decide what to do about it.

## Watch the Queue, Not Just the Rail

### Counting Ahead

Glance at the line every few minutes and count heads. Groups of coworkers usually order together and arrive in one wave. A line that grows while you cook is telling you the bottleneck is behind the window, not in front of it.

### Finding the Bottleneck

The bottleneck is whatever station everything waits on. It is usually one of four things:

1. A single fryer or burner that every item passes through
2. Portioning and assembly, when one person is doing both
3. Payment, when the same person taking money is also plating
4. A component that ran out and is being cooked from scratch mid-rush

Only one of these is the real constraint at any moment. Fixing anything else changes nothing.

#### The Cheapest Fix Is Usually Not Equipment

Separating money from food is often the single biggest speed gain a truck can make, and it costs nothing but a second set of hands. The next biggest is prepping more before the doors open.

## Pacing and the Lull

> The work during the rush was mostly decided an hour before the rush started.

Rushes are predictable. Office lunch crowds land in a narrow window; brewery and event traffic builds later and stays longer. Use the slow period before a known rush to fill hot wells, portion proteins, and stage containers, following the batch cadence in [[cooking-and-holding]].

During the rush, protect your pace. Call out an honest wait time to people joining the line. Customers forgive a wait they were told about and resent one they discovered.

### Talk in Short, Fixed Phrases

Small kitchens run on call-and-response. Ticket in, called out loud. Item fired, confirmed. Order up, acknowledged. Fixed phrasing means nobody has to interpret anything while their hands are full.

## Service Data Is Menu Data

What you notice at the window should feed back into the menu. The item that always adds two minutes is a design problem, not a cook problem. The item nobody orders is taking up prep time and cooler space. Bring those observations to [[building-a-core-menu]] and to [[specials-and-rotation]], where a weak item can be tested and replaced.
